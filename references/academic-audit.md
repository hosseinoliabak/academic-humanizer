# Academic audit and author feedback

Use this guide during the audit step. Its starting examples come from author feedback supplied during
an editing session. The revisions are candidates for review, not claims about which wording a human
or a model would use. Future feedback can extend this guide after its meaning and scope are clear.

## Apply feedback without changing the scholarship

Keep the author's academic register and established terminology. When suggestions offer Academic,
Business, Simple, Casual, or Creative variants, prefer the Academic version when its meaning fits.
Check it against the sentence's actual meaning; a category label is not evidence that two phrases are interchangeable.
Do not substitute a more ornate word merely to make the prose sound scholarly.

Audit all supplied concerns, even when the original construction is legitimate. State whether each
one was revised, retained for a stated reason, or remains unresolved. Ask a focused question when
choosing a revision requires an unknown technical relationship or an author preference that matters.
Continue work on unaffected text while that point is pending.

If feedback includes a detector's likelihood, impact label, or authorship explanation, record it as
user-supplied feedback. Do not repeat it as a verified property of the phrase or infer authorship
from a rhetorical question, a declarative sentence, formality, or a three-item list.

## Positive reference examples

Read [the positive examples](positive-examples.md) when applying the initial author's profile. Use
them to identify strengths to preserve, not just faults to remove. Direct, unadorned declarations and
neutral technical wording can suit academic writing. Retain them when they establish the setting or
introduce a topic clearly; judge rhythm and repetition across connected sentences.

The supplied positive and negative feedback sometimes assigns different labels to similar features,
including simple syntax and precise wording. Do not turn either label into a universal rule. Consider
the sentence's function, meaning, and fit with its paragraph. Do not infer authorship or predict a
detector result. Interpret the positive "Journalistic Style" label as neutral, informative academic
reporting in these examples, not a request to turn the manuscript into news writing.

Positive style feedback does not verify a claim. Keep the evidence review separate, and preserve
meaning-bearing qualifiers and alternatives. A statement's directness is not a reason to strengthen
its claim, broaden its population, or remove uncertainty. Report useful wording retained as well as
changes made. The positive examples are style references, not reusable claims for other manuscripts.

## Question framing and impersonal tone

Distinguish a genuine unanswered research question from a rhetorical question that only introduces
a topic. When the author objects to question framing, prefer a declarative statement of what the
study investigates. Preserve the question's uncertainty, actors, and conditions. Do not turn "whether
X can help" into "X helps." Formal research questions can remain when the author or document requires
them; note the reason in the audit.

**Supplied sentence**

> Can information learned from other customers help a newly participating customer with a short
> legitimate traffic history and no local attack labels?

**Candidate academic revision**

> This thesis examines whether information learned from other customers can help a newly participating
> customer whose legitimate traffic history is short and whose local attack labels are unavailable.

The revision changes the framing, not the unanswered question or either customer condition. Check
the surrounding paragraph for another "This thesis examines" sentence and combine or reposition it
when appropriate. Do not introduce "we" or a personal anecdote just to create a personal tone.

## Predictable syntax and robotic formality

Inspect adjacent sentences for repeated subjects, openings, clause shapes, and similar lengths.
Replace empty abstractions with the actor, action, or scope relationship already supported by the
text. Vary sentence structure where that clarifies the argument. Keep a straightforward declaration
when it expresses the point well; do not make every sentence complex or add artificial imperfections.

Necessary qualifications are part of academic writing. Recast stiff scope statements without deleting
their limits or changing a lack of a claim into a positive or negative finding.

## Three-part lists

Inspect each list item for a distinct meaning before treating the list as padding. Remove an item
only when it is redundant and doing so preserves the content. When all three matter, retain them,
recast the syntax, or distribute them across sentences according to their relationship. Do not replace
specific exclusions with a vague label such as "commercial aspects." Do not expand the list to four
items just to avoid a triple.

**Supplied sentence**

> It does not claim to establish the viability, cost, or capacity of a complete commercial protection
> service.

**Concerns to address together**

- The detached pronoun and repeated declarative pattern, if present in the paragraph.
- The author's concern about rigid formal phrasing.
- The three parallel nouns, each of which names a substantive scope exclusion.

**Candidate academic revision**

> The thesis makes no claim to establish the viability of a complete commercial protection service,
> nor does it make that claim about the service’s cost or capacity.

This candidate retains all three exclusions and changes the sentence pattern. It keeps the limit on
what the thesis claims, rather than asserting what the investigation did or did not establish.
Separate sentences can also be appropriate when they connect better to the paragraph. The number of
sentences is not a target.
Do not change "capacity" to "scalability" or "viability" to "profitability" without supporting evidence.

## Academic phrase alternatives

### Shared experience

The supplied example identifies **shared experience** and offers **collective experience** as its
Academic alternative. Prefer that alternative when the sentence really describes experience held
collectively. Retain uncertainty, such as "may benefit," when it belongs to the claim.

**Candidate in the supplied context**

> Although collective experience may benefit this customer, its legitimate workload may differ from
> the workloads used to train the shared detector.

When "experience" obscures the intended mechanism, a phrase already supported by the text, such as
"information learned from other customers," may be clearer. Do not infer raw-data pooling, federated
learning, model-parameter exchange, or a training architecture from the word "collective." Do not
replace the technical phrase "shared detector" merely because it contains the word "shared."

### Highlighted the need

**Supplied vocabulary feedback:** "highlighted the need"; impact label "Very high"; the screenshot
reports "9.8 x" greater use by an AI model than a human in its document dataset. Record this as
supplied feedback, not an independently verified frequency, authorship probability, or training weight.

**Supplied Academic alternative:** "underscored the necessity."

Inspect the original phrase for generic emphasis. Prefer the Academic alternative only when it fits
the full sentence's meaning and the author's plain academic register. It is not an automatic or
author-approved replacement: "underscore" already appears among the skill's vocabulary concerns,
and "necessity" may make a stronger claim than the original wording supports. Do not replace one
formulaic emphasis phrase with another merely because a suggestion is labeled Academic.

Preserve the intended emphasis, degree of need, tense, and relationship to the supporting evidence.
When recasting the sentence, state the supported actor, observation, or action directly if that makes
the point clearer. Do not invent a requirement, mechanism, or finding, or upgrade an indication of
need into proof of necessity. Either expression may remain when its context justifies it, with the
reason reported in the audit.

**Status:** Phrase recorded; final replacement remains context-dependent because no full sentence
was supplied. Obtain the sentence when revising that occurrence rather than inventing a context.

## Connected prose and feedback records

Use a natural mixture of sentence lengths. Keep familiar information near the start and the point
that the next sentence develops near the end when the actual relationship supports it. Avoid
mechanically repeating the same word or adding causal links that the content does not establish.

When applying the author's saved Practical Research/UDM directives, use the more specific rules in
that guide rather than treating the general wording above as sufficient. In that author profile:

- Plan a small group of connected sentences together. Put familiar information in the topic position
  and the point to emphasize in the stress position at the end.
- Check every adjacent sentence pair. If the next sentence opens with a term or idea from the current
  sentence, move that term or idea to the end of the current sentence rather than leaving it buried
  in the middle. Do not weaken this explicit placement rule to "near the end." This does not require
  every sentence to repeat a term from the previous one or invent a connection between unrelated ideas.
- Let information order determine the opening. An introductory phrase, dependent clause, participial
  phrase, or transition can provide natural variation. Do not use a fixed rotation or require every
  sentence to begin with its grammatical subject or an "-ing" phrase.
- Keep the subject close to its verb and express the action in the verb when that improves clarity.
  Keep connected reasoning together rather than splitting it solely to shorten sentences.
- Use simple English with a calm academic tone. Give each paragraph one main purpose and keep
  qualifications beside the claims they limit. Read the saved guide for the rest of that author's
  directives, including technical explanations and focused refreshers.

These are the initial author's recorded preferences, not universal requirements for every academic
author. Apply another author's explicit guide when it differs. A style guide settles how to arrange
prose; it cannot identify an unseen antecedent for "therefore" or "instead." Inspect accessible
manuscript context before asking for it, and request only the missing claim or contrast when needed.

For each future example, record the original span, the author's concern, a candidate revision, the
meaning that must remain, and its review status. Keep pending candidates distinct from accepted
preferences. Detector explanations belong to the feedback record, not to an empirical evidence base.
Do not add private manuscript passages to a public repository unless the user authorized that scope.

## Technical precision and logical relationships

When feedback flags "Technical Jargon," "Sophisticated Clarity," or "Mechanical Precision," separate
necessary terminology from unnecessarily dense presentation. Keep terms such as dataset, service
provider, federated learning, decision threshold, and outlier exposure when they name the actual
concepts. Improve their placement and explanation rather than replacing them with vague synonyms.
Identify the term retained and the surrounding wording revised in the audit.

Check the relationship carried by each transition. "Therefore" needs a supported inference from
preceding text, and "instead" needs an identifiable alternative. Without that context, mark the
relationship as context-dependent rather than inventing an antecedent or declaring the transition
redundant. "While" may express simultaneous conditions; retain that relationship when it matters.
Check the actual grammar rather than copying a detector's grammatical explanation as an observation.

Preserve what a sentence does as well as its facts. An applicability condition, motivation, research
question, purpose statement, method description, and finding make different claims. Do not replace a
conditional relevance statement with a general capability statement merely because the latter is
technically true. Keep a result relationship when recasting words such as "resulting."

Interpret "Lacks Creative Grammar" or "Lacks Creativity" as a request to inspect rhythm, information
flow, and repetitive framing. Do not introduce grammatical errors, casual asides, metaphors, or
unnecessary complexity. Recast abstract thesis signposting when a supported actor or purpose makes
it clearer, without inventing a research procedure or a finding.

Preserve modality and the direction of conditions. "Need not" removes a requirement; it does not
report the actual deployment. "Only where" limits when a use is allowed; do not weaken it to a
possibility or turn it into proof that the condition has been satisfied. Keep planned actions distinct
from completed work. Preserve source-provenance requirements and explicit proxy labels together.

The [worked feedback examples](feedback-examples.md) record nine supplied sentences, their feedback
categories, candidate academic revisions, and preservation checks. Read the relevant cases rather
than treating the examples as a substitution dictionary. All candidates remain subject to author review.

## Audit delivery

For short passages, a compact table can show location, supplied concern, proposed action, and
preservation check. Follow it with the revised passage and a brief change report. Keep the manuscript's
paragraph structure unless the user authorizes restructuring. Never claim the revision passed a
detector or matches an author's preferences before that has been tested or reviewed.
