# Academic audit and author feedback

Use this guide during the audit step. Its starting examples come from author feedback supplied during
an editing session. The revisions are candidates for review, not claims about which wording a human
or a model would use. Future feedback can extend this guide after its meaning and scope are clear.

## Apply feedback without changing the scholarship

Keep the author's academic register and established terminology. When suggestions offer Academic,
Business, Simple, Casual, or Creative variants, prefer the Academic version when its meaning fits. Check it against
the sentence's actual meaning; a category label is not evidence that two phrases are interchangeable.
Do not substitute a more ornate word merely to make the prose sound scholarly.

Audit all supplied concerns, even when the original construction is legitimate. State whether each
one was revised, retained for a stated reason, or remains unresolved. Ask a focused question when
choosing a revision requires an unknown technical relationship or an author preference that matters.
Continue work on unaffected text while that point is pending.

If feedback includes a detector's likelihood, impact label, or authorship explanation, record it as
user-supplied feedback. Do not repeat it as a verified property of the phrase or infer authorship
from a rhetorical question, a declarative sentence, formality, or a three-item list.

## Question framing and impersonal tone

Distinguish a genuine unanswered research question from a rhetorical question that only introduces
a topic. When the author objects to question framing, prefer a declarative statement of what the
study investigates. Preserve the question's uncertainty, actors, and conditions. Do not turn "whether
X can help" into "X helps." Formal research questions can remain when the author or document requires
them; note the reason in the audit.

**Supplied sentence**

> Can information learned from other customers help a newly participating customer with a short
> legitimate traffic history and no local attack labels?

**Candidate academic revision**

> This thesis examines whether information learned from other customers can help a newly participating
> customer whose legitimate traffic history is short and whose local attack labels are unavailable.

The revision changes the framing, not the unanswered question or either customer condition. Check
the surrounding paragraph for another "This thesis examines" sentence and combine or reposition it
when appropriate. Do not introduce "we" or a personal anecdote just to create a personal tone.

## Predictable syntax and robotic formality

Inspect adjacent sentences for repeated subjects, openings, clause shapes, and similar lengths.
Replace empty abstractions with the actor, action, or scope relationship already supported by the
text. Vary sentence structure where that clarifies the argument. Keep a straightforward declaration
when it expresses the point well; do not make every sentence complex or add artificial imperfections.

Necessary qualifications are part of academic writing. Recast stiff scope statements without deleting
their limits or changing a lack of a claim into a positive or negative finding.

## Three-part lists

Inspect each list item for a distinct meaning before treating the list as padding. Remove an item
only when it is redundant and doing so preserves the content. When all three matter, retain them,
recast the syntax, or distribute them across sentences according to their relationship. Do not replace
specific exclusions with a vague label such as "commercial aspects." Do not expand the list to four
items just to avoid a triple.

**Supplied sentence**

> It does not claim to establish the viability, cost, or capacity of a complete commercial protection
> service.

**Concerns to address together**

- The detached pronoun and repeated declarative pattern, if present in the paragraph.
- The author's concern about rigid formal phrasing.
- The three parallel nouns, each of which names a substantive scope exclusion.

**Candidate academic revision**

> The thesis makes no claim to establish the viability of a complete commercial protection service,
> nor does it make that claim about the service’s cost or capacity.

This candidate retains all three exclusions and changes the sentence pattern. It keeps the limit on
what the thesis claims, rather than asserting what the investigation did or did not establish.
Separate sentences can also be appropriate when they connect better to the paragraph. The number of
sentences is not a target.
Do not change "capacity" to "scalability" or "viability" to "profitability" without supporting evidence.

## Academic phrase alternatives

The supplied example identifies **shared experience** and offers **collective experience** as its
Academic alternative. Prefer that alternative when the sentence really describes experience held
collectively. Retain uncertainty, such as "may benefit," when it belongs to the claim.

**Candidate in the supplied context**

> Although collective experience may benefit this customer, its legitimate workload may differ from
> the workloads used to train the shared detector.

When "experience" obscures the intended mechanism, a phrase already supported by the text, such as
"information learned from other customers," may be clearer. Do not infer raw-data pooling, federated
learning, model-parameter exchange, or a training architecture from the word "collective." Do not
replace the technical phrase "shared detector" merely because it contains the word "shared."

## Connected prose and feedback records

Use a natural mixture of sentence lengths. Keep familiar information near the start and the point
that the next sentence develops near the end when the actual relationship supports it. Avoid
mechanically repeating the same word or adding causal links that the content does not establish.

For each future example, record the original span, the author's concern, a candidate revision, the
meaning that must remain, and its review status. Keep pending candidates distinct from accepted
preferences. Detector explanations belong to the feedback record, not to an empirical evidence base.
Do not add private manuscript passages to a public repository unless the user authorized that scope.

## Audit delivery

For short passages, a compact table can show location, supplied concern, proposed action, and
preservation check. Follow it with the revised passage and a brief change report. Keep the manuscript's
paragraph structure unless the user authorizes restructuring. Never claim the revision passed a
detector or matches an author's preferences before that has been tested or reviewed.
